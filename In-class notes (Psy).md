### Bias
- ==One potential bias in this study is…, which could affect the results by…==
- ==The way participants were selected could introduce bias because…==
- ==The researchers controlled for bias by …==
- ==Demand characteristics could influence behavior by causing participants to…==

### Causality
- One factor that reduces our ability to draw causal conclusions is…
- The study’s internal validity is strengthened/weakened by…
- Since this is a correlational / quasi-experimental study,
- Questions about causality remain because
- The evidence partially supports a causal relationship, but
### Change
- The results of the study changed over time in that…
- Long-term change cannot be determined since …
- If the study were replicated today, the results might differ because…
- Changes in participants’ behavior may be due to maturation rather than …
- Overall, the findings suggest change under these conditions, however …
### Measurement
- Measurement in this study focused on…
- The measurement appears reliable because …
- Using this type of measure is appropriate because…
- Construct validity may be limited because…
- This method may oversimplify the variable by…
### Responsibility
- One ethical issue raised by this study is…
- Because the study involved children/animals/individuals with limited capacity,
- The benefits of the research may/may not outweigh the ethical costs because…
- If this study were conducted today,
- Long-term effects on participants should be considered because…

- Domino causality
    - What happens if your alarm clock doesn't go off?
        Well, you wake up late, then you hurry to get out of the house, and in your rush, you forget to take your psychology project with you; you aren't able to participate in the class assessment and are kept after class by the teacher, you miss your break and have to go immediately to your next class, you missed seeing the love of your life during the break, you get a text in your next class that your love is upset that you didn't have time for them - and your relationship is over.
        So, you can see that this chain of events is quite complex.  It would be ridiculous to argue, "My boyfriend/girlfriend broke up with me because my alarm clock didn't go off!"  Yet, often in psychology, the way that cause and effect is discussed on exams is similar in simplicity.
    - **The serotonin hypothesis**
        For example, the serotonin hypothesis argues that a lack of serotonin causes depression. One has to try to determine what are the steps that lead from a serotonin deficit to depression. What does a serotonin deficit do? How does one get a serotonin deficit? When we look at modern research on depression, we find that one of the effects of high levels of stress is an increase in the stress hormone cortisol. This leads to an increase in an enzyme called MAO-A.  This enzyme breaks down neurotransmitters, including serotonin. So, if we look at the bigger question, is cortisol the ultimate "cause" of depression rather than serotonin?  If so, this may explain why simply treating serotonin levels in many depressed patients is not effective.
    - **ACEs**
        Nadine Burke Harris writes about ACEs - Adverse Childhood Experiences - and their effect on physical health.  But is it possible to say that abuse and neglect in childhood _cause_ physical health problems?  Well, it is.  But it is not a linear relationship.
        One study that looked at the potential effect of childhood neglect was **[Michael Meany](https://student.thinkib.net/psychology/page/26506/meaney-et-al-1988-)**.  In his study of rats, he found that neglect led to memory loss in adult mice.  But it was not that simple.
        The mother rat’s nurturing behavior leads to the activation of the glucocorticoid receptor gene (GR, also known as NR3C1), resulting in a lowered stress response. The stress of being taken away from the mother leads to _down-regulation_ of the expression of the GR – that is, the expression of the gene is decreased. This epigenetic effect leads to the mice's inability to regulate glucocorticoid levels. Long-term high levels of cortisol lead to hippocampal cell death. This cell death affects the rats’ ability to recall spatial information.

## Extraneous variables (p.3-4)
For each of the following scenarios, identify the participant bias or the carryover effect that compromises the internal validity of the study. Suggest how the researchers could have better controlled the study.

1. A psychology professor tells students that a new learning app is designed to improve memory. Students who use the app later perform better on memory tests than those who don't.
    **Expectancy effect,** placebo, participant bias + researcher bias
    **Suggested Control:** Do not directly state the aim of the study to the participants, Double/ single blind approach.

2. A study investigates whether caffeine improves reaction time. The same participants are tested three times in a row, and their performance improves each time.
    Practice effect
    **Suggested Control:** Use different participants for each trial condition. counter balance. bigger sample. Double / single blind approach
3. Participants are given a sugar pill but are told it is a powerful new antidepressant.  
    Many report feeling happier and more energized after taking it.
    Placebo effect
    **Suggested Control:** Include a control group and a blind procedure to isolate actual treatment effects from expectation-driven effects.

4. A criminal psychologist finds that a participant identifies a person in the lineup who matches a photo they were shown in a pre-test of their memory skills.
    **Interference effect**
    **Suggested Control:** Use different, non-overlapping materials for pre-tests and real tests or space out the sessions to reduce memory confusion.

5. During a long problem-solving task, participants begin to lose concentration and make more mistakes toward the end of the session.
    fatigue effect
    **Suggested Control:** Break the session into shorter intervals with rest periods 

6. In a health survey about drug use, participants underreport their usage because they want to appear responsible.
    social-desirebility effect
    **Suggested Control:** The survey should be anonymous.  This will decrease the likelihood that participants will be dishonest. 

7. Some participants in a clinical trial know they are in the control group and deliberately exaggerate their symptoms to undermine the results.
    Screw-you effect
    **Suggested Control:** Use double-blind random assignment so participants don’t know whether they are in the control or experimental group

8. A group of people takes a sugar pill that they are told may cause nausea. A few of them begin to feel queasy even though the pill contains no active substance.
    Nocebo effect**Suggested Control:** Avoid suggesting specific side effects or use neutral language during consent to minimize expectation-driven negative outcomes.

### Theory
Cultur: role of bilingualism 
Investigating bilingual advantage
Research on the bilingual advantage uses **theoretical** **triangulation**, integrating psychology, neuroscience, linguistics, and education, providing a well-rounded perspective on how language experience affects cognition. It also employs **method triangulation**, using experiments, neuroimaging, and computational models to explore how bilingualism impacts the brain.
Bilinguals differ in age of acquisition, proficiency, language use, and linguistic environments. Differences in socioeconomic status (SES) and education can confound results, as SES influences cognitive development.
Most studies are **cross-sectional**, making it difficult to determine causation rather than correlation. More recent studies address previous methodological issues by using larger sample sizes, standardized tasks, and **longitudinal designs** to track cognitive changes over time.
Meta-analyses show mixed results, suggesting publication bias.
Brain imaging studies show structural and functional differences in bilinguals, but these do not always translate to measurable cognitive advantages.

### PET (Positron Emission Tomography) scans
In addition to MRIs and fMRIs, other imaging techniques can be used to study the brain. Although knowing about MRIs and fMRIs is enough for DP Psychology, it is good to know that there are other options.
+ used to observe metabolic processes in the brain by injecting a radioactive tracer into the bloodstream
	+ Tracer will then make its way to the brain
	+ If a particular brain area is more active, more glucose will be needed there
	+ When more glucose is used, the PET scanner detects the radioactive tracer.
+  Scan (usually 30mins - 2hrs) produces a multi-colored image showing which brain parts are the most active
	+ Color of each dot shows the intensity of the energy signal.
+  Advantage: 
	+ allows participants to perform psychological tasks while the researcher observes brain activity
+ Disadvantage: 
	+ requires an injection with a small amount of radioactive material.  Although this will not cause harm to the participant, it is still an invasive practice and raises ethical concerns
	+ slow and has relatively poor resolution. So, although it does indicate where brain activity is taking place, it is not as clear as more modern technology like the fMRI.

For an example of PET scanning, see the study by Setiawan (2013)
