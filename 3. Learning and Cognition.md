Schema theory explains why people may have false or distorted memories - something that neither the Multi-Store Model nor the Working Memory Model can do.

A large number of experimental studies have been conducted to test the model. However, many of the studies are artificial and lack **ecological validity**.

There is **biological support** for the theory that our brain categorizes information. **Mahon et al.** (2009) found that information about living and non-living objects is shuttled to different parts of the visual cortex - even in blind participants. These findings suggest that our brains automatically sort information and classify it like schema theory predicts.

Schema theory has been applied in abnormal psychology (therapy for depression and anxiety), relationships (theories of mate selection), and health psychology (health campaigns to change unhealthy behaviors). It is a **robust** theory with many applications across many fields of psychology.

**Cohen** (1993) argued that the concept of schema is too vague and hypothetical to be useful. Schema cannot be observed or measured - so the theory's **construct validity** may be challenged.

The theory **helps to predict behavior**. For example, we can predict what types of information will be best recalled when given a list of words. Trends, such as omitting information not highly relevant to the individual, are commonly seen in individuals recalling a news story. However, we cannot predict exactly what a specific individual will recall.

  

**Schema theory: Linking to contexts**

In addition to the simple examples in this chapter about how schema theory works, schema theory is relevant to the different contexts of this course. **Cognition and learning**: Schema theory explains memory distortion and provides a framework for explaining how we learn. **Development**: Language development is based on schema theory.

**Health and wellbeing**: People with mental illness often have maladaptive schema—that is, a pervasive self-defeating or dysfunctional theme developed during childhood or adolescence and elaborated throughout one's lifetime, which takes the form of a belief about one's self or the world. Human relationships: When we enter a relationship, we interpret its success through experience. Some theories argue that our experience with our parents forms an **internal working model** that influences our future relationships.

  

**Change: Soft determinism**

Schema theory is important in understanding **change**.

Schema theory argues that behaviors can be changed by assimilation or accommodation.  The cognitive approach argues that behavior can be changed by changing how we think and/or process information.  This is often called soft determinism - the idea that I can change my behavior if I think differently.

This directly contrasts with **biological determinism**, which argues that biological factors will determine my behavior and that this is mostly beyond my control.

In 1992 and then 2003, Corkin carried out an MRI scan of HM’s brain to see the extent of the damage. It was possible to see that parts of HM’s temporal lobe including the **hippocampus** had the most damage. Damage to the hippocampus explains the problem of transferring short-term memory to long-term memory as this is the area where the neurotransmitter **acetylcholine** is believed to play an important role in learning and the formation of memories.

The following is a summary of the key findings:

- Memory is a complex, specialized system involving multiple brain regions.

- The hippocampus plays a critical role in converting information from short-term memory to long-term memory.

- Short-term memory is not stored in the hippocampus as HM was able to retain information for a while if he rehearsed it.

- Since HM was able to retain some memories of events that happened long before his surgery it indicates that the medial temporal region is not the site of permanent storage but rather plays a role in the organization and permanent storage of memories elsewhere in the brain.

- Implicit memory contains several stores - for example, procedural memory, emotional memory, and skills and habits. Each of these areas is related to different brain areas.
