# _Research methods_
## Experiments
- #### True experiments (characteristics)
    - A researcher manipulates an independent variable, measures a dependent variable, and controls all other variables.
    - Participants are randomly allocated to conditions.
    - Researchers test a hypothesis.  They either retain or reject a null hypothesis.
    - The data is quantitative.  The researcher carries out statistical analysis to determine if the results are significant.  Qualitative data may also be collected.
    - The procedure is highly standardized, allowing for replication to establish the reliability of the results.  
    
- #### Quasi experiments (characteristics)
    - The researcher does not manipulate the independent variable being investigated in the study; it is naturally occurring.
    - Participants are not randomly allocated to conditions. Instead, they are assigned to groups based on existing characteristics.
    - While quasi-experiments strive to control for confounding variables, this is often more challenging than in true experiments, resulting in lower internal validity.
    - Because the IV is naturally occurring, quasi-experiments have higher ecological validity.
    - Causality cannot be determined from quasi-experiments.
# _Research considerations_
### Demand characteristics
- cues in a study that inadvertently communicate to participants what behavior or responses are expected or desired by the researcher
- expectancy effect
- screw-you effect
- reactivity

**Controlling demand characteristics**
1. **Independent samples design**.  
    ~ By not being exposed to both conditions, participants are less likely to figure out the goal of the experiment
2. During the debriefing, ask the participants if they know what was being tested. If they answer yes, this may influence the results.
3. **Deception**  
    **~** may lead to ethical problems if the deception leads to undue stress or harm to the participant.
### Order effects (carryover effects)
- occurs in a repeated measures design. Effects of one condition influence performance in a later condition.
- fatigue effects
- interference effect
- the practice effect
==**Controlling demand characteristics**==
1. Counterbalancing
2. pauses between conditions
3. filler activities  
    ~ clears the participants' short-term memory.  
    ~ controls for interference effects

### Internal Validity
~ extent to which a study can confidently demonstrate a **cause-and-effect relationship** between IV and DV, without the influence of confounding variables or bias
**Threats to internal validity**
- Participant biases
- Demand characteristics
- reactivity
- placebo/nocebo effect
- social desirability bias
- Carryover effect/order effects
    - interference effect
    - practice effect
    - fatigue effect
### External Validity
~ extent to which the results of a study can be **generalized** beyond the specific conditions of the experiment — to other people, settings, times, and measures  
_Do the findings apply to the real world or just to this specific study?_
- ecological validity
- population validity
- ethics
**Construct validity** - how well your test/manipulation represents concept you're trying to study.
you really measuring what you say you are measuring?  
**Internal validity** is about how sure you can be that the manipulation of the IV caused the change in the DV.  
These two types of validity are linked. Poor construct validity undermines internal validity. If you're not accurately manipulating or measuring the concept of interest (construct validity), any observed cause-and-effect relationship (internal validity) is questionable.
If you use a "stress" questionnaire that actually measures fatigue, and find a link to test performance, your causal claim about stress is invalid because you weren't really measuring stress.  
Good construct validity strengthens internal validity. When your variables truly represent what they're supposed to, your conclusions about causality are more trustworthy.  
**A study can have high internal validity but low construct validity.** You might show that variable X causes a change in variable Y — but if X and Y don't truly represent the constructs you care about, your results are precise but meaningless.
# _Data analysis_
- psychologists want to see the middle of data rather than extreme/outliers
- middle of data = more generalization
### ==**Frequency tables**==
- organize + summarize data
- how often each value/category appears
- useful in descriptive statistics - shows trends
### ==**Bar graph**==
- represent categorical/discrete numerical data
- height of each bar represents frequency/value of specific category/condition in study
### ==**Histograms**==
- represents continuous data by showing distribution of dataset
- displays numerical data divided into intervals - bars touching each other to show continuousness
    - peak of histogram = mode (most common)
    - asymmetrical histogram = skewness in data
### ==**Box and whisker plots**==
- good way to show distribution of a dataset
- Valuable for identifying medians, variability, skewness, outliers, and comparing distributions across multiple groups
- Visual display of the five-number summary
    - minimum value ($(Min)_x$)
    - lower quartile (Q1)
    - median (Q2)
    - upper quartile (Q3)
    - maximum value ($(Max)_x$)
- “box” represents the interquartile range (IQR) which shows the middle 50% of the data
    - $IQR = Q3 - Q1$
    - measures spread/variability of middle 50%
    - provides clearer pic of data distribution while minimising outlier influences
    - Line inside indicates the median
- “whiskers” extend from the box to the smallest and largest data point within 1.5 times the IQR
- Any points outside this range are plotted as outliers
    - $Q1 - (IQR \times 1.5 + Q3)$
- The concept of skewness in box and whisker plots
    - Symmetric distribution (no skew)
        - indicates data is evenly distributed around the center
        - median is approx in the center of the box
        - whiskers are roughly equal in length on both sides
    - positive skew/ right skew
        - most of data is concentrated in the lower range of values w/ a few larger values pulling the distribution to the right
        - median is closer to the lower quartile (Q1)
        - whisker on right side is longer than left whisker
    - negative skew/ left skew
        - most of data is concentrated in the higher range of values w/ a few smaller values pulling the distribution to the left
        - median is closer to the upper quartile (Q3)
        - whisker on left side is longer than right whisker

### Scatterplots
- allows for observation of relationships between two continuous variables
- helps asses the s**trengths and direction of correlation**
    - positive correlation (increase in one variable corresponds to increase in another)
    - negative correlation (increase in one variable, the other one decreases)
    - no pattern = no meaningful relationship between the two variables
- Helps detect **outliers** → helping determine whether an extreme case influences the findings
1. collect numerical data for the two variables of interest
2. plot each data point on a graph, w/ one variable on the x-axis and the other on the y-axis
3. examine the pattern of data points, to determine whether a relationship exists between the variables
4. Line of best fit can be added to quantify the relationship (if a trend appears)

- x-axis typically represents the IV

- effective for communicating findings to policymakers, educators, healthcare professionals bc of clear representation of data w/o statistical knowledge

## Interpreting data
### Descriptive statistics
- summarize and organize data revealing meaningful patterns
- communicate findings clearly, compare groups, identify trends b4 conducting more complex inferential analyses
- provides insight into key characteristics of a dataset
    ### Distribution shape
    **Normal distribution curve (bell curve)**
    - describes how data is distributed in a population
    - symmetrical, bell-shaped curve
    - most of the data points cluster around the mean
    - fewer data points the further away from the mean in either direction
    - x-axis shows values in terms of standard deviation (σ) from the mean
    - ~68% within one standard deviation of the mean, ~95% within two, ~99.7% within three
    - extreme data are rare and fall at the tails of the bell curve
    ### Central tendency (mean, median, mode)
    ==**Mean**== (average)
    - add all values in a dataset, divide by the total number of values
    - useful when data is normally distributed and free from extreme outliers (that could skew the results)
    **Median** 
    - middle value of a dataset when arranged in ascending order
    - useful when dealing with skewed distributions or data with extreme outliers (that could distort the mean)
    - if number of values is even, average the two middle numbers to find the median
    ==**Mode**==
    - most frequently occurring value in a dataset
    - useful for categorical data
    - not influenced by outliers or by other values in the data set
    - used when average is not possible due to non numerical data
    e.g. finding school’s fav subject. Finding the “average” class is not possible, therefore the most frequently chosen would be looked at.
    ### Variability (range, variance, standard deviation)
    - provides insight on consistency/diversity of behaviors, traits, or test scores are within a population
    - High variability = greater difference in individuals, low variability = similar performance/behvr
    - Important for assessing individual differences, determining effectiveness of treatments, making reliable predictions
    ---
    **Variation ratio**
    - most useful for categorical data
    - determines the variance of data
    - shows percentage of the data that was not the value of the mode
    - lower VR = mode is more representative of the data set
    - e.g. 11/20 students choose psychology as fav class, therefore $VR = 1 - (11/20) = 0.45$ (shows high level of variance in data
    - With only 0.55 of the students saying that psychology is their fav class, shows that there is a high level of variance in the data
    [![](https://student.thinkib.net/media/ib/psychology/images/vr.jpg)](https://student.thinkib.net/media/ib/psychology/images/vr.jpg)
    **Standard deviation (SD)** 
    - best suited for normally distributed numerical data
    - shows how much data points deviate from the mean
    - represents average difference of scores from the mean
    - **smaller SD** = data points are closely clustered around the mean (low variability)
    - **Larger SD** = a wider spread of data points (high variability).
    **Interquartile range (IQR)**
    - ideal when there are outliers
    - measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data
    - particularly useful when extreme values might distort the overall picture
    
    ### Measurement (types of data):
    **Ordinal data**
    - number that isn’t really a value, but a rank
    - e.g. Likert scale - asks participants to **rank a response on a scale** of 1 - 5
        - The 1 - 5 values are "positions" on the scale, they do not have a true numeric value.
        - Choices are subjective
        - Numbers can be used in either direction, e.g. 1 = never on one question, then later in a survey, 1 = always
    - Some statisticians argue guesses/estimates are ordinal data
    **Nominal data**
    - refers to **categorical data** consisting of labels/names without numerical value/order
    - Often result of choices made between fixed options e.g. yes, no, maybe,
    - e.g. best type of therapy (in ur opinion), ur fav color
    Analyzing nominal datab = calculating **mode** + **variation ratio**
    ### Eliminating outliers
    Researchers should never eliminate data because they think it compromises the significance of their study. 
    If outliers are eliminated from a data set, the choice needs to be justified and explained in the analysis of the report.
    **Practical justification**
    - Data can be discarded when it is clear that there is a problem with the data that is not related to its value.
    E.g. If a person in sample who clearly did not understand directions and did the task incorrectly, could be removed from the sample.
    - If the response is not clear. 
    E.g. If asked to estimate the speed two cars were traveling when they "smashed" into each other, if a participant writes "between 100 and 120 km/hr",
    It would not be appropriate to find the median value - 110 - and include this in the calculations.
    - If (during debrief) it is clear that it will lead to demand characteristics
    E.g. Participant had already done the experiment, didn't understand directions, guessed study’s aim
    **Statistical justifications**
    - determine if they mathematically "qualify" as outliers
    Low outliers: $Q1 - (1.5 \times IQR)$
    High outliers: $Q3 + (1.5 \times IQR)$
    1. find median - start with lowest value then count to the halfway point
    2. find lower quartile (Q1) and upper quartile (Q2) - find the mid-point of each data set
    3. find Interquartile range - (Q3 - Q1)
    4. find low outliers $Q1 - (1.5 * IQR)$
    5. find high outliers $Q3 + (1.5 * IQR)$
### Inferential statistics
- allows researchers to draw conclusions about populations based on sample data
- aims to determine whether **variables are related** and whether **groups differ significantly**

### Correlation analysis 
- examines the strength and direction of relationships between two continuous variables
- Strong correlation suggests two variables move together but doesn’t imply causation
- measures **association**

**correlation coefficient** (r)
- tells us how strongly two variables are related and in what direction
- value between -1 and 1.
    - **Positive correlation (r > 0):** When one increases, the other also increases (e.g., more study time → higher grades).
    - **Negative correlation (r < 0):** When one increases, the other decreases (e.g., more video games → lower grades).
    - **No correlation (r ≈ 0):** No clear pattern; the variables are unrelated.
The closer r is to 1 or -1, the stronger the relationship.
If r = 0.4 it is a **moderate** positive correlation.
However, to know if this is a significant correlation, consult statistical tables that show the minimum r value needed for significance based on **the sample size** and **desired level of significance**.
For example:
- With **n = 10**, an r of **0.4** is _not_ significant at p = 0.05.
- With **n = 50**, an r of **0.4** _is_ significant at p = 0.05.
### Significance testing 
- e.g. t-tests
- assesses whether differences between groups or conditions are statistically meaningful rather than due to random variation
- evaluates **causality and group differences**
- Accepted probability level is p < 0.05
    - meaning there is 5% (or less) chance that results are due to chance
    - means that results are **significant** which is determined by finding "critical values"

### Regression analysis
- examines relationships between variables and makes predictions
- regression determines how much one variable influences another while controlling other factors
- helps researchers make conclusions based on real-world data

**Multiple regression analysis** 
A researcher might want to know whether stress levels predict academic performance while accounting for factors like sleep and study habits. multiple regression alalysis can show which factors have the strongest effect on grades.
e.g. psychology class studying what affects students’ happiness levels collects data on:
- Hours of sleep per night
- Amount of time spent on homework
- Number of hours spent socializing
by using regression analysis, they can determine which factor has the strongest impact on happiness.

### **Hypothesis testing**
**null hypothesis** (H0) = assumes no effect or no difference
H0: There is no significant difference in recall from a list of words between individuals who get eight hours of sleep and those who get four hours of sleep b4 a test.
H0: Regular physical exercise does not significantly reduce symptoms of depression compared to individuals who do not exercise.
H0: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy does not significantly reduce anxiety symptoms compared to a control group receiving no therapy.
- null hypothesis doesn’t say that there is no difference; it predicts that there won’t be a "significant difference"
- If null hypothesis is rejected, results are **significant**
    Inferential statistics determine the probability of the result occurring if the null hypothesis is true
**alternative hypothesis**(H1) = suggests presence of an effect or difference
H1: There is a significant difference in recall from a list of words between individuals who get eight hours of sleep and those who get four hours of sleep b4 a test.
H1: Regular physical exercise significantly reduces symptoms of depression compared to individuals who do not exercise.
H1: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy significantly reduces anxiety symptoms compared to a control group receiving no therapy.
**two-tailed hypothesis**
- used when researchers are interested in detecting any significant difference, regardless of direction
- tests whether the effect is either greater than or less than a certain value
- more powerful when there is a strong theoretical reason to expect a specific direction
- risks missing an effect in the opposite direction
**one-tailed hypothesis**
- predicts the direction of the effect
- specifies whether one group is expected to perform better or worse than the other
- only considers one direction and ignores the possibility of an opposite effect
- more conservative and widely used when the researcher wants to detect any difference, regardless of direction.
### **The value of p-values**
**p-value** (probability value)
- statistical measure used in hypothesis testing to assess the strength of evidence against the null hypothesis
- quantifies likeliness of observed differences/effects occurred purely by chance
---
small p-value (≤ 0.05)
- statistically significant
- suggests strong evidence against the null hypothesis, leading researchers to reject it.
Large p-value (> 0.05)
- result is not significant
- suggests weak evidence against the null hypothesis, meaning there is not enough support to reject it.
However, failing to reject the null hypothesis does not prove it is true; it simply means that the data does not provide sufficient evidence to conclude otherwise
One common misconception is that a small p-value proves the alternative hypothesis is true. In reality, it only suggests that the null hypothesis is unlikely. Another misunderstanding is assuming that a large p-value confirms the null hypothesis when, in fact, it may simply indicate insufficient data or **statistical power** to detect an effect.
In research, the most common threshold for statistical significance is 0.05 (5%), meaning there is a 5% chance of observing the given result if the null hypothesis were true. Lower p-values, such as 0.01 (1%), are used in high-stakes studies. For example, consider a study testing whether a new drug improves memory. If the p-value of the experiment is 0.03, it means that if the drug truly had no effect, results this extreme (or more) would be expected only 3% of the time by random chance. Since 0.03 is below 0.05, the null hypothesis would be rejected, suggesting that the drug likely has a real effect.

Types of triangulation
**Methodological Triangulation**
Researchers use different research methods to study the same phenomenon. For example, a researcher might use both qualitative methods (e.g., interviews) and quantitative methods (e.g., surveys).
**Data Triangulation**
Researchers use different sources of data to confirm findings. For instance, a psychologist might collect data from multiple participants (e.g., a child, his parents, his friends, his teacher)
**Researcher Triangulation**
Multiple researchers or observers interpret the data, analyze results, or conduct interviews to reduce bias.
**Theoretical Triangulation**
This type uses multiple theoretical perspectives to analyze and interpret the data. For example, a cognitive and a biological perspective to provide a more holistic understanding of the behavior.

Evaluation of the case study method
**Strength**
- Case studies provide rich data.
- The use of triangulation increases the findings' credibility.
- Allows researchers to investigate cases that could not be set up in laboratories.
- Case studies may generate new theories.
**Limitation**
- Case studies cannot be replicated, and a single case study cannot be generalized to a wider population.
- The longitudinal nature of case studies may risk a lack of objectivity and researcher bias.
- The reliance on memory when reconstructing the case history could be subject to distortion.
- Longitudinal case studies may suffer from participant attrition - a high dropout rate of participants.
